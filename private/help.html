1 · What this is

Tickr Lab watches a list of stocks and scores them on how their prices are behaving and how the underlying companies are doing. It is a screener: it narrows a list down to the few names worth a closer look. The assistant deliberately never offers a verdict; the Action column does carry one, but it is the output of a plain rule set you can read in full in section 12 — a consistent reading of today's numbers, not a prediction.

Stocks are grouped into portfolios — a stock can belong to several — and the picker at the top left switches between them. Prices update after each close; the company numbers behind Quality refresh about once a day.

2 · Reading the table

There are 49 columns, arranged into groups you can collapse: Info, Scores, Price Momentum, Chart, Short-term %, Long-term %, Relative, Trend, Volume, Size and Fundamentals. The Columns menu turns whole groups on and off, and remembers your choice against your account.

Getting around

  • Each row in the Columns menu has a jump arrow that scrolls that group into view — faster than dragging a scrollbar across 3,400 pixels.
  • ← → scroll the table sideways, Shift with them moves a page at a time, and Home / End jump to either end.
  • Click any column header to sort by it; click again to reverse.

Price Momentum

Three columns that describe the momentum score and how it has moved:

  • Mom. — the score now, 1 to 10.
  • Past Mom. — the same score as it stood at the end of a horizon you choose, on the 0–100 scale. The Past picker in the bar offers one week, two weeks, one month, three months and six months.
  • Mom. Delta — now minus then, in score points. Positive means the stock has strengthened against the fixed scale, not merely against its neighbours.

The Delta is only coloured, and the arrow beside Mom. only appears, once the move clears a threshold that scales with the horizon. The median name travels about three points in a week and fifteen in six months, so a single fixed cut-off would mark half the table at one horizon and nearly all of it at another.

These read across time, which is only meaningful because the score is absolute. While momentum was ranked against the list, a change could come from a ticker being added rather than from the stock. Now it cannot.

What is hiding behind a cell

  • Hover a company name for a card showing every column for that row at once — useful when the value you want is off to the right.
  • Hover a score (Overall, Mom. or Qual.) to see the factors behind it, each with its weight and how this stock scored on it.
  • The 90d column is a sparkline of the last 90 sessions. Read the shape, not the level — each line is scaled to its own high and low, so two lines are not comparable.
  • A small chevron beside the Momentum rating means the score has moved further than the chosen horizon's threshold: green improving, red fading. No chevron means the move was inside the noise for that horizon.

3 · How scoring works

Every stock carries three scores out of ten. Momentum describes how the price has behaved. Quality describes the company behind it — growth, margins, valuation. Overall blends them, 65% momentum and 35% quality, and clicking that column sorts the list by it.

Every score is absolute. Each factor is measured against a fixed scale rather than against the other stocks on your list, which means three things worth knowing:

  • A number means the same thing over time. A momentum of 70 in a weak quarter is the same 70 as in a strong one, so this month's score can be compared with last year's.
  • Adding or removing a ticker changes nobody else's score. Your scores describe the stocks, not the company they happen to be keeping.
  • In a bad market the whole list scores low, which is the honest answer. A ranked score cannot say that — the best of a bad lot still comes out near the top.

The scales are not guesses. Each factor's centre is the median measured across the price archive at dates spanning 2011 to 2026, so a typical stock in a typical period sits near the middle. The return factors are divided by volatility first, which makes them dimensionless and is what allows a fixed scale to mean anything at all.

Nothing is pinned at the ends. Each factor uses a curve that approaches 0 and 100 without reaching them, so two very strong stocks still separate. An earlier version used straight cut-offs and flattened a third to a half of the list onto a floor or a ceiling, which is a factor that cannot tell anything apart.
Missing data is dropped, not counted as zero. If a factor cannot be computed for a stock — usually too little price history — it is removed and the remaining weights are rescaled to fill the gap. A stock is never punished for a number nobody has.

4 · The eight momentum factors

These are the sliders behind Weights → Custom. The percentage beside each name is its share under the Default weighting.

12-1 momentum

20%

The last twelve months of return, excluding the most recent month, divided by how much the stock normally swings.

Skipping the final month is deliberate. Over a year, strength tends to continue; over a few weeks it tends to give some back. Leaving the last month in would mix the two effects and blunt both.

Raise it to favour names with a long, established run. Lower it if you care more about what is happening now than what happened last autumn.

6M return (risk-adjusted)

18%

Six-month return divided by realised volatility.

The division is the important part. A 40% gain in a stock that normally drifts is a stronger signal than the same 40% in one that swings that much routinely — without adjusting, the score would simply reward whatever is most volatile.

Raise it for a medium-horizon view that still leans on established moves. Lower it to let the one-year and three-month factors decide.

3M return (risk-adjusted)

17%

The same measure over three months.

This is the shortest horizon treated as more is better. Below about three months the evidence flips towards reversal, which is why the one-month factor further down is inverted rather than simply weighted less.

Raise it to react sooner to something that has started working. Lower it to be less easily impressed by a good quarter.

% from 52-week high

10%

How far below its highest point of the last year the stock is trading.

Sitting near a 52-week high is itself a form of strength: there is nobody left holding at a loss and waiting to sell into a recovery. A stock 40% below its high has to climb through all of that.

Raise it to favour names already at the top of their range. Lower it if you are hunting for recoveries rather than leaders.

Trend regime

10%

Which side of its 200-day average the stock is on, and whether the 50-day has crossed the 200-day in the last twenty sessions.

Unlike the return factors this one is a category, not a ranking. A fresh cross upward scores full marks, an established uptrend slightly less, a long downtrend little, and a fresh cross downward nothing. It is the one factor that says which way the tide is running rather than how fast.

Raise it to insist on trend confirmation. Lower it if you are willing to be early, before the averages agree.

Consistency

10%

What share of the last twelve months closed higher than the month before.

Two stocks can post the same annual return, one by grinding upward most months and one on a single enormous week. This separates them. It is the closest thing here to a measure of how comfortable the ride was.

Raise it to prefer steady climbers over one-jump wonders. Lower it if you do not care how the return arrived.

1M reversal

8%

The one-month return, inverted — the biggest recent movers score lowest here.

This is the only factor that runs backwards, and it is not a mistake. At a one-month horizon the evidence points to reversal rather than continuation, so a name that has just jumped 30% is more likely to give some back than to repeat. Treat it as a brake, not a verdict: it never outvotes the longer factors, it just stops the score chasing whatever moved last week.

Raise it to be more cautious about anything that has just run. Set it to zero — as the Trend preset does — to chase strength wherever it appears.

RSI timing

7%

Where the 14-day Relative Strength Index sits, scored on a curve that peaks around 70–75.

Not more-is-better. A reading in the low 70s is the sweet spot — strong but not stretched. Below 30 scores poorly because the stock is falling, and above 85 the score drops away again because it is overextended and prone to a snapback.

Raise it to weight entry timing more heavily. Lower it if you intend to hold through the noise anyway.
Weights do not have to total 100. Only their relative sizes matter — the score rescales them for you, which is what the second number beside each slider shows. Setting every slider to 10 is the same as setting them all to 1.

5 · Presets and Custom

Trend

Shorter horizons, a stronger trend requirement, and no reversal brake at all. It wants whatever is working right now and accepts that some of it will give back.

Steady

The year-long factor and month-to-month consistency lead, and the three-month factor is cut to 10. A name has to have been working for a while, and to have done it repeatedly.

Custom

Eight sliders, saved to your account, so the setting follows you to another browser or machine. Choosing Custom starts from a copy of Default rather than a blank slate, and Copy Default puts it back at any time.

Changing weights changes your view, not the data. The screener and the analysis page each remember their own choice. The nightly email, the assistant and the stored snapshot always use Default, so a score quoted anywhere outside your own screen means the same thing to everyone. Whenever a non-default weighting is in force, the Momentum and Overall columns are tinted, and an exported CSV names the weighting in those column headers.
A word of caution. Sliders make it easy to keep adjusting until the list agrees with something you already believe, then to read that back as evidence. The reordering figure shown when you pick a preset is there to keep you honest about how much you have moved. If a weighting only looks right for today's list, it is probably fitted to it.

6 · The Quality score

Where Momentum reads the price, Quality reads the company. Six factors, all absolute rather than ranked against the list — a 30% margin is a 30% margin whoever else is on your screen.

FactorWeightMeasures
Earnings growth25Latest quarter against the same quarter a year earlier
Revenue growth20The same, on the top line
PEG20Price/earnings set against the growth rate — cheapness relative to growth
Profit margin15Net income as a share of revenue
Forward P/E10Price against next year's expected earnings
ROE10Return on equity — profit against the capital behind it

Companies without earnings

Three of those factors — earnings growth, PEG and forward P/E — describe earnings, and mean nothing for a company that is losing money. For a loss-maker they are excluded rather than scored zero, and the remaining factors are re-weighted to cover the gap.

This matters more than it sounds. The data feed will happily report a healthy-looking PEG for a company losing hundreds of millions, and taken at face value it once rated such a company 8 out of 10.

Sometimes there is no Quality score at all. If less than 40% of the factor weight has usable data, the column shows a dash rather than a number, and Overall falls back to Momentum alone. A score resting on one factor is not a score. ETFs and funds show a dash for the same reason — they have no revenue or earnings of their own.

7 · The analysis screens

The Analysis page holds nine filtered views. They are built from the raw numbers rather than the composite scores, on purpose: a screen that just re-sorts the ranking tells you nothing the table did not.

Every threshold was set by running the candidate against the real list. A screen that returns nothing is a dead box, and one that returns a third of the list is not a signal.

ScreenWhat it looks for
Momentum improvingMomentum score up 5 points or more over a fortnight

Reporting in 14 daysA catalyst and a risk in the same event

OverextendedRSI above 75 and more than 12% above the 50-day average
The first two are the ones a ranked table cannot give you. Everything else describes where a stock stands; those describe where it is going. A name climbing from the middle of the pack is the interesting case — by the time it reaches the top of the ranking, the move has already happened.

8 · One stock in full

Clicking a company name opens that stock on its own page, in a new tab, with a price chart and every column the screener holds. The symbol at the top is also a picker — click it to jump to any other stock without going back.

The chart

  • Ranges from one week to the full archive, which for most names runs back to 2006.
  • 50-day and 200-day averages can be switched on independently. Each is drawn from enough extra history to start at the left edge rather than partway across.
  • Volume sits below the price, each bar green or red by whether that session closed above the one before.
  • RSI is its own panel with the 70 and 30 lines marked. Turning it on makes the chart taller rather than squeezing the price.
  • Move the pointer across for a dotted crosshair, the date on the axis, and a readout of price, day change, volume and RSI at that point.
The y-axis switches to a log scale when a range spans more than about four-fold, and says so in the caption. Over twenty years a linear axis flattens everything but the last few months into a line along the bottom.

9 · Asking questions

Ask puts a question to the same data in plain English — "which names have a margin above 20% and are within 10% of their high", or "what is improving fastest". The whole table is given to the assistant, so it is answering from your actual numbers rather than from memory.

What it will not do

  • No buy, sell or hold verdicts. Not a disclaimer bolted on the end — it will not produce one, for the same reason it will not make up a number.
  • It says when something is missing and points you at what is there instead, rather than inventing a plausible answer.
  • "Will it go up" is unknowable, and it will say so rather than guessing.
  • General company knowledge is allowed, but flagged as background rather than presented as something your data shows.
There is a daily limit — three questions a day for members, sixty for the owner. It counts against your account rather than your browser, resets at midnight UTC, and a question that fails on a bad request does not consume one.

10 · How fresh the data is

The bar shows when the data was last pulled and the trading day it covers. Everyone sees the same stored snapshot, so two people reading the screener are reading the same numbers.

Two kinds of refresh owner only

  • Refresh re-pulls prices and recomputes the ranking. Seconds, and the company numbers are left as they were.
  • Refresh all additionally re-pulls every company profile — sector, market cap, margins, growth. It runs for about thirteen minutes, in batches, because the data provider only allows so much per minute.

A full refresh runs on its own every evening, after the close, so the list is current each morning without anyone touching it. While one is in progress every open page shows a notice, and the values already on screen stay put rather than blanking out.

A warning triangle beside a symbol means that stock's most recent price is older than the freshest one on the list. Usually it trades on an exchange with a different calendar, not that anything is broken.

11 · Your account

What is remembered

Which column groups you have collapsed, and which momentum weighting each page is using, are stored against your account rather than your browser — so they follow you to another machine, and are not shared with whoever else uses this computer.

Emails

  • A short welcome when the account is created.
  • A password reset link if you ask for one. It works once and expires in thirty minutes; asking for a new one cancels the old.
  • Replies to anything you send through Contact, which reaches the owner directly.

Signing in

Change your password from the ⋯ menu; doing so signs out your other devices, which is the point. If you are locked out after too many attempts, it clears itself after fifteen minutes.

Something wrong, or a ticker you would like added? Use Contact — it comes from your account, so a reply reaches you directly.

12 · The Action column

The Action Model column group shows the whole derivation, left to right: Type (Established, Early or ETF) · Trend (the price-trend reading — Breakdown, Downtrend, Below 200D, Above 200D or Strong uptrend: whether you may) · Entry (Clean, Clean near high, Extended or None: whether now) · Fund. (Strong, OK, Weak, or — when the data makes no case: how much conviction) · Guards (earnings soon, thin history — blank for most stocks, and meaningful when it is not: wait) · and the Action itself (Strong Buy · Buy · Buy with Risk · Hold · Avoid · Sell Immediately). The group collapses as one unit from the Columns menu. The model is a list of plain rules checked top to bottom — the first rule that matches is the answer, and hovering an Action cell shows the one rule that fired. The Sell, Avoid and Hold rules always sit above the Buy rules, so no amount of good news can buy a stock whose trend is broken. Backtested over 307,875 stock-days since 2008, the tiers order downside correctly (the worst tenth of 3-month outcomes: about −12% for the Buy tiers against −25% for Sell Immediately, before and after 2020 alike) — but median returns are flat across tiers. It manages risk; it does not predict winners.

Company Type

ETFs (no company financials) are scored on technicals alone. Everything else earns an establishment score out of 7: market cap ≥ $100B +2 (≥ $20B +1) · profitable +1, with a margin ≥ 5% +2 · positive free cash flow at a ≥ 5% margin +1 · forward P/E between 0 and 40 +1 · ROE ≥ 10% +1. Score 5+ (or $200B of size on its own) is Established; below that, Early — judged on growth, gross margin, cash burn and short interest, never on P/E or earnings growth, which mean nothing without earnings. A company migrates the day it earns it. A blank value never satisfies any rule, in either direction.

The rule lists (Balanced defaults)

Established: Sell Immediately on a breakdown (more than 10% below the 200-day average and still falling: 1M < −8% or 3M < −15%) → Avoid in a downtrend, or below the 200-day with weak fundamentals, or on heavy-volume selling more than 30% off the high → Hold if below the 200-day, no trend data yet, under 200 sessions of history, earnings within 7 days, or extended (1M > +25%, or >15% above the 50-day, or RSI > 72) → then Strong Buy (>10% above the 200-day, a clean entry — within −3%…+8% of the 50-day with RSI 45–65 — near the high and strong fundamentals) → Buy (clean entry, decent fundamentals, less than 20% off the high) → Buy with Risk (clean entry, fundamentals unproven but not weak) → Hold.

Early: the same shape with a shorter leash — breakdown at −5%/−5%, weak fundamentals below the 200-day is an immediate Sell, merely being below the 200-day is Avoid rather than Hold, short interest above 25% is Avoid, and Buy needs strong growth (revenue > 30%, gross margin > 50%, positive FCF) in a >10% uptrend. Early is never Strong Buy unless a profile enables it. ETF: the technical rules alone.

One rule set, for now

The thresholds below are the model's fixed defaults (the "Balanced" set). User-editable profiles — presets, per-user overrides, an admin-edited house set — were built and then deliberately shelved for now to keep the screener simple; the engine still reads every threshold from configuration, so they can return without a rewrite. The CSV export carries Type, the four state columns, Action and the flag naming the rule that fired.
