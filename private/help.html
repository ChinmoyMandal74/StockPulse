1 · What this is

Tickr Lab watches a list of stocks and scores them on how their prices are behaving and how the underlying companies are doing. It is a screener: it narrows a list down to the few names worth a closer look. The Signal column does carry a verdict, but it is the output of a plain rule set you can read in full under The Signal column — a consistent reading of today's numbers, not a prediction.

Stocks are grouped into themes — a stock can belong to several — and the picker at the top left switches between them. They are also cut by sector, industry and size, each with a page of its own. Prices refresh about every half hour through the US session; the company numbers behind Quality are re-pulled on a rotation over about a week.

Why this exists

A brokerage terminal gives you four hundred indicators, streaming quotes and no opinion — the synthesis is your job, every time, forever. Tickr Lab is the opposite bet: one opinionated, fully-inspectable pipeline over a small universe you chose. Its defining feature is not any single number but that every signal names the one rule that fired — every Signal cell carries the rule that produced that word, not just the word. There is no "why exactly does this say Strong?" anywhere else; here it is the whole design.

The second bet is institutionalised honesty. The backtests charge trading costs and hold out a period before looking; ideas that measured as noise are recorded so they stay dead; the trend line on a stock page refuses to claim any history the price bars cannot prove; and the nightly email says "prices only — fundamentals not re-pulled" rather than letting stale numbers pass as fresh. Even the watchlist carries deliberate ballast — faded names nobody is excited about — because a list made only of winners flatters every idea tested against it.

And the third is deliberate absence. No streaming quotes, no options chains, no order entry, no five-hundred-stock scanners: this is a daily-cadence research instrument, not a trading cockpit. It decides what deserves your attention and shows you exactly why — the last mile belongs to your broker.

2 · Reading the table

There are around ninety columns, arranged into groups you can collapse: Info, Scores, Signal, Chart, Short-term %, Long-term %, Relative, Trend, Volume, Size, Fundamentals and Ownership. The View menu (top left) decides what the table shows. Standard is every column, grouped, and lets you switch whole groups on and off; your choice is remembered against your account.

Not every column is switched on for everyone — the site owner hides some site-wide, and those stay hidden whatever view or screen you pick.

Column views

A view is a named set of columns you can switch to with one click from the View menu. Symbol and Name are always shown, and the columns keep the table's order.

  • Starter views (Trend & returns, Fundamentals, Signal) are shared by everyone. The lock beside them means only the site owner can change them — use the + beside one to copy it into your own views and change the copy.
  • Your views: + New view opens a list of every column with a search box; tick what you want, name it and save. It starts from whatever the table is showing, so trimming is quicker than building. The pencil edits a view, the bin deletes it. Up to ten.
  • The view you last used is the one the screener opens in. Filters on a column the view hides still apply (the filter strip marks them), and if the table is sorted by a hidden column a note beside the menu says so.

Getting around

  • In Standard, each group in the View menu has a jump arrow that scrolls that group into view — faster than dragging a scrollbar across 3,400 pixels.
  • ← → scroll the table sideways, Shift with them moves a page at a time, and Home / End jump to either end.
  • Click any column header to sort by it; click again to reverse.

The headline strip

Under the table, a slow crawl of headlines published in the last 12 hours for the stocks the table is showing — pick a theme, a sector, a Signal or a column filter and the strip narrows with it — newest first, with each stock's move today. Hover it (or press pause) to stop it; click a ticker for the stock page or a headline for the article. Hide it with its ×, and bring it back from ⋯ → News ticker — the choice is remembered. These are stored headlines, fetched a few stocks at a time as the data refreshes, so they are recent rather than live.

Filtering any column

The Filter button in the bar opens a row of boxes under the column headers — one per column. Type in as many as you like; a row has to pass all of them. The button shows how many are in force, and a strip above the table lists them with an × on each and a Clear filters button.

  • Numbers: 5 means at least 5 · >5 <5 <=5 =5 !=5 · 2..8 for a range · 10B 500M for big numbers. A blank cell never matches. Scores read the 1–10 rating you see; Next Earn counts days, so ..14 is within two weeks.
  • Text (symbol, name, themes, guards): the text it contains · =NVDA for exact · !memory to exclude · chips|memory for either.
  • Lists (type, trend, entry, signal…): pick from what is actually in the table, with counts. Sector, Industry and Signal are the same filters as the Sector, Industry and Signal pickers in the bar.
  • Esc clears the box you are in. Hiding a column keeps its filter working, and the strip says so. Filters reset when you reload the page; whether the row is open is remembered.

What is hiding behind a cell

  • Hover a company name for a card showing every column for that row at once — useful when the value you want is off to the right.
  • Hover the Qual. score to see the factors behind it, each with its weight and how this stock scored on it.
  • Click a Sector, Industry or Size cell and the table narrows to it. Click the same value again to clear it — it is the picker in the bar, in a second place. Those three columns deliberately have no hover card: the card would land under the pointer on its way to the click.
  • The 90d column is a sparkline of the last 90 sessions. Read the shape, not the level — each line is scaled to its own high and low, so two lines are not comparable.
On a touch screen there is no hover card and no score tooltip. A tap would have to be spent dismissing one before you could reach the link underneath, so nothing is wired at all — tap the name to open the whole stock page instead, or use the phone page.

Two clocks on every row

  • Price pulled is when that stock's price was last fetched, which genuinely differs row to row — prices are pulled in slices. It turns amber past a day.
  • The tooltip names the newest bar beside it, because "we asked" and "something came back" are different facts. Read the two together.
  • Days held, in the Signal group, is how long the signal has stood. It prints ≥ until the run has actually been seen to change, because the count only starts from when the column shipped and a bare number would overstate it.

3 · The Quality score

Where the return columns read the price, Qual. reads the company behind it. Six factors, all absolute rather than ranked against the list — a 30% margin is a 30% margin whoever else is on your screen. That is worth knowing for three reasons: a number means the same thing this year as last, adding or removing a ticker changes nobody else's score, and in a bad market the whole list scores low, which is the honest answer. A ranked score cannot say that — the best of a bad lot still comes out near the top.

FactorWeightMeasures
Earnings growth25Latest quarter against the same quarter a year earlier
Revenue growth20The same, on the top line
PEG20Price/earnings set against the growth rate — cheapness relative to growth
Profit margin15Net income as a share of revenue
Forward P/E10Price against next year's expected earnings
ROE10Return on equity — profit against the capital behind it

Companies without earnings

Three of those factors — earnings growth, PEG and forward P/E — describe earnings, and mean nothing for a company that is losing money. For a loss-maker they are excluded rather than scored zero, and the remaining factors are re-weighted to cover the gap.

This matters more than it sounds. The data feed will happily report a healthy-looking PEG for a company losing hundreds of millions, and taken at face value it once rated such a company 8 out of 10.

Sometimes there is no Quality score at all. If less than 40% of the factor weight has usable data, the column shows a dash rather than a number. A score resting on one factor is not a score. ETFs and funds show a dash for the same reason — they have no revenue or earnings of their own.
Missing data is dropped, not counted as zero. A factor that cannot be computed for a stock is removed and the remaining weights are rescaled to fill the gap. A stock is never punished for a number nobody has.

4 · Narrowing it down

The Screens menu beside View holds ready-made screens. One click sets the filters, the sort and the columns that explain the result — the filters appear in the filter row and the strip above the table, so a screen never hides what it means. Each shows how many stocks match right now, for the theme in view.

  • Change any of its filters and the menu shows the screen as edited. Clear screen puts the table back exactly as it was before — your sort, filters and view.
  • Theme still applies on top: pick a theme first and a screen searches only that list.
  • Screens are set by the site owner (hence the lock). They replace the old Analysis page, whose seven screens are all here.
  • Star the ones you use and they move to a Favourites group at the top of the menu. The star is yours, kept against your account; the screen itself is still shared. It moves rather than copies, so the menu does not get longer for using it.

The pickers in the bar

Beside Screens, five quick cuts that stack with each other and with everything above:

  • Theme, Sector, Industry and Size. Industry nests inside Sector — it only offers industries that are actually in view — and changing the sector drops an industry that no longer applies. Each menu carries live counts, and says how many rows have no value yet, so counts that do not add up are explained rather than puzzling.
  • Signal filters to one signal.
  • Signal move answers which way, not just whether: Moved, Upgraded, Unchanged or Downgraded since the previous session. A stock with no previous verdict matches none of them — nothing to compare against is a different fact from having held — and the menu counts those separately.
  • These are not remembered between visits, deliberately: a filter surviving a reload is a table missing rows for no visible reason.

5 · One stock in full

Clicking a company name opens that stock on its own page, in a new tab, with a price chart and every column the screener holds. The symbol at the top is also a picker — click it to jump to any other stock without going back.

Beside it, the chips — sector, industry, size and every theme it belongs to — and each one opens that group's page. Then two pills, Google Finance and Yahoo Finance, which open this stock there in a new tab.

The chart

  • Ranges from one week to the full archive, which for most names runs back to 2006.
  • 50-day and 200-day averages can be switched on independently. Each is drawn from enough extra history to start at the left edge rather than partway across.
  • Volume sits below the price, each bar green or red by whether that session closed above the one before.
  • RSI is its own panel with the 70 and 30 lines marked. Turning it on makes the chart taller rather than squeezing the price.
  • Move the pointer across for a dotted crosshair, the date on the axis, and a readout of price, day change, volume and RSI at that point.
The y-axis switches to a log scale when a range spans more than about four-fold, and says so in the caption. Over twenty years a linear axis flattens everything but the last few months into a line along the bottom.

Similar stocks

At the foot of the page, up to six other companies in the same industry, the ones closest to this one in market cap, with price, today, a year, market cap, forward P/E and the signal. Each row opens that stock; the vs pill opens the pair on the compare page.

  • The page names the rule it used. Where an industry holds fewer than three other companies it widens to the sector and says so, rather than quietly answering a different question.
  • Funds have no peer group. An ETF is not a company, so it neither gets a table nor appears in anyone else's. Nor does a stock whose sector and industry have not arrived yet — company profiles are re-pulled on a weekly rotation.
  • A dash under Fwd P/E is a company with no positive forward earnings, or one that reports none. A multiple off a loss is arithmetic, not cheapness, and printing it as a negative number would make the loss-maker look like the cheapest name in the column.
  • It is not a ranking and not a shortlist. Nothing is marked as better; it is who else is on this screen, measured the same way.

Earnings history

One bar per quarter, oldest on the left, green where the company beat the estimate and red where it missed, with a folded table of the estimate, what was reported, the surprise and whether it landed before or after the bell.

  • A quarter with no estimate is neither a beat nor a miss — it is drawn as a hairline and left out of the count, so the summary reads "9 of 11" rather than quietly counting it.
  • The scale is robust rather than absolute. A surprise is a ratio against the estimate, so a near-zero estimate makes it enormous; the tallest bar is the 90th percentile of what is there, and anything past it is drawn full height and says so on hover.
  • Depth varies a lot. Some names hold six years, the typical one reaches back to 2020, and the header says which.

SEC EDGAR

At the foot of the page, the company's own filed statements — quarterly or annual, back to 2007 for a long-listed name — with revenue, margins, earnings, cash flow and the balance sheet. Every row links to the filing it came from on sec.gov.

  • These will not always match the numbers elsewhere on the page. They are on the company's fiscal calendar and as reported in the filing named on the row, where the Fundamentals card is trailing-twelve-month and restated to today. Two different questions, not a disagreement.
  • A dotted value was computed here, not filed. No company files a fourth quarter — it files three and then a full year — and a cash-flow statement is year-to-date, so those quarters are the difference between the cumulative periods either side. Hover for the reason.
  • Later filings restate earlier periods, and each row shows the most recent version.
  • A fund has no section at all, because it files no financial statements. Neither do companies reporting under international standards rather than US GAAP.
Nothing on that card feeds the Signal or the screener. It is there to be read, not to move anything — a deliberate line, so that the rules stay driven by one consistent set of numbers.

6 · Sectors, industries, sizes and themes

Every one of those four cuts has its own page, and they are all the same page — a group of stocks summarised. Reach one from the chart arrow beside any row in the Theme, Sector, Industry or Size picker, or from the chips under the symbol on a stock page.

What the page shows

  • A curve — the group held in equal dollars from the start of the window, against the S&P 500 and the Dow 30 on the same convention. Ranges from a month to a year.
  • What it is made of — a sector breaks into its industries, an industry into size bands, everything else into sectors. The bar is market cap and the number beside it is companies, and where those two disagree is the interesting part: "Technology up 18%" means something different if two names are 60% of it. Each row opens that group.
  • Leaders and laggards over the window the range buttons chose, so the list and the curve above it can never tell different stories.
  • Fundamentals against the whole screen — medians, not averages, because one 300× multiple drags a mean somewhere no company in the group actually sits.
  • The stocks themselves, biggest first.
A forward P/E at or below zero is left out of the median, and financials are left out of gross margin — both counted and said rather than quietly dropped. A multiple off a loss is arithmetic, not cheapness.

7 · The pivot view

Pivot view in the bar counts the screen by two things at once — sector against verdict, theme against trend state, size against sector. Rows and columns are yours to choose, and every number is a count of stocks, their total market cap, or a return.

Getting an answer out of it

  • Click any cell and the stocks behind it are listed underneath, with fifteen columns and a link to each. Row and column totals open the same way.
  • A sector row opens into its industries from the caret beside its name, and the children add up to the parent exactly — the page says so, with the sum.
  • Last 12 months or Last 12 years across the top turns it into a track record: one column per period, newest first, each cell the group's equal-weight return.
  • Saved pivots keep the question — both axes, the measure, the shading and the filter — never the answer, so opening one tomorrow counts tomorrow's screen.
The page explains its own totals rather than leaving you to trust them. A stock in two themes is counted once per theme, so the cells can add to more than the universe — it says by how many and why. Stocks with no value for a cut sit in a — bucket rather than being dropped. With a period across the top, a company is absent from periods it did not trade rather than counted as a flat zero, so an early column is an average over fewer companies, and the note says how many have all twelve.

8 · Two stocks side by side

Compare in the bar opens two stocks together — every field, with the distance between them. The screener's button carries whatever is on top of your current view; the stock page's Compare ▸ carries that stock and whatever you have overlaid on its chart. Two, never more: a difference is a pairwise thing, and three columns of ninety fields is a table again.

Reading it

  • The chart is rebased, both lines starting together at the first session the two share, so the gap between them is the relative performance. Two raw share prices on one axis would flatten the cheaper one into the floor.
  • A ratio strip underneath — above 100, the first stock is ahead of where it started.
  • Bars on every row show the size of a gap at a glance, each drawn as its share of the larger of the pair. Where a value is negative, zero moves to the middle.
  • Hide matching rows folds away the fields where both read the same, with the count on the button.
Nothing is marked as the winner, and that is deliberate. How far apart two numbers are is a fact; which of them is better is not. Some differences are withheld rather than computed — across two currencies, or off a loss — with the reason on the row.

9 · Alerts

Alerts in the bar watches one stock for one thing and tells you the next time you open the site. Up to five at a time. Six kinds: a price level, a moving-average cross, an Signal change, an RSI level, a 52-week extreme and a big day.

Things worth knowing before you set one

  • They are not real-time, and the page says so in amber. Prices land on a schedule, so an alert fires when new data arrives — up to about half an hour later in the session, and the next morning outside it. This is a what changed on my list feature.
  • A level is not an event. "Price above 150" is true every round once it is true, so only the crossing fires — otherwise you would hear about it every half hour for ever.
  • Set one whose condition is already true and it arms quietly, reporting the next change instead of announcing a crossing that did not happen. The row says Arming.
  • A gap counts. 140 to 160 overnight never touched 150, and it still fires.
  • Pausing forgets where it was. A resumed alert arms again rather than firing on a move it never saw.
No email, anywhere in this feature. Delivery is a badge on the bar you can ignore. It is also never called a price target — a target is a forecast wearing a decimal point, and this is a level you chose.

10 · How fresh the data is

The bar shows when the data was last pulled and the trading day it covers. Everyone sees the same stored snapshot, so two people reading the screener are reading the same numbers.

Prices refresh on their own about every half hour through the US session, and a full refresh — prices and the company numbers with them — runs after the close, so the list is current each morning without anyone touching it. Nothing here is live: a price arrives when the next scheduled pull brings it, which can be half an hour after it moved. While a full refresh is in progress every open page shows a notice, and the values already on screen stay put rather than blanking out.

Company numbers — sector, market cap, margins, growth — are on a slower rotation: a share of the list each night, so every company comes round about once a week. That is why a newly added stock can sit with blanks in the Fundamentals and Size groups for a few days, and why the Industry of a recent addition may be empty for a while.

A warning triangle beside a symbol means that stock's most recent price is older than the freshest one on the list. Usually it trades on an exchange with a different calendar, not that anything is broken. The Price pulled column gives the same thing per stock, with the time.
If something is wrong across the board, a coloured banner appears at the top of the screener saying so. Dismiss it with its ×; if the message is later updated, it comes back, so a follow-up reaches you even after you have waved the first one away.

11 · Your account

What is remembered

Which column groups you have collapsed, which view you were last in and how you had the table set up are stored against your account rather than your browser — so they follow you to another machine, and are not shared with whoever else uses this computer.

Your own themes

You can keep personal themes — your own named lists built from the stocks already in the screener. Click the star on any row to add or remove that stock, and find your lists at the bottom of the theme picker, where each one can be renamed or deleted. They are private to your account (the owner can review them), they follow you between machines like your column layout, and they never add a stock to the system — only the owner can do that, so ask through Contact if a ticker you want is missing. Up to ten lists; the dashed badges in the Themes column are yours, the solid ones are shared.

Emails

The app sends you almost nothing, on purpose. There is no welcome mail and no digest, and alerts are a badge rather than an inbox.

  • A password reset link if you ask for one. It works once and expires in thirty minutes; asking for a new one cancels the old.
  • Replies to anything you send through Contact, which reaches the owner directly.
  • New posts, but only if you ask for them — see below.

The blog, and the list

Posts at tickrlab.com/blog are public: no account needed, nothing gated. The box at the foot of each one adds you to the mailing list, which is confirmed opt-in — you get one email asking you to confirm, and if you ignore it nothing happens. Every message carries a one-click unsubscribe, and your browser and mail client can both act on it. Nothing else is ever sent to that address.

The watermark

Data pages carry a faint, repeating watermark of your account email. It does not get in the way of reading — it simply means anything captured from your screen identifies the account it came from. The data here is for your personal use, not redistribution.

Signing in

Registration needs an invite code. With one you are in straight away; without one the guest preview is open to everyone. Change your password from the ⋯ menu; doing so signs out your other devices, which is the point. If you are locked out after too many attempts, it clears itself after fifteen minutes.

Sign in with Google works too, where it is switched on. The first time, it asks for the same invite code after Google comes back — the code is the door, whichever way you arrive at it — and after that you go straight in. If the address matches an account you already have, the two link up and your password keeps working.

The guest preview

Anyone can try the screener without an account, from Try as guest on the sign-in page. It is twenty real stocks, chosen to span every sector, size band and verdict rather than to be the famous ones — the whole table, every column and every verdict, on those twenty. What a guest does not get: personal lists, alerts, the pivot, compare, the studio and the research pages.

Something wrong, or a ticker you would like added? Use Contact — it comes from your account, so a reply reaches you directly.

12 · The Signal column

“Signal” here means the output of the fixed rules described below — a mechanical reading of the table, not a recommendation tailored to you.

The Signal column group shows the whole derivation, left to right: Type (Established, Early or ETF) · Trend (the price-trend reading — Breakdown, Downtrend, Below 200D, Above 200D or Strong uptrend: whether you may) · Entry (Clean, Clean near high, Extended or None: whether now) · Fund. (Strong, OK, Weak, or — when the data makes no case: how much conviction) · Guards (earnings soon, thin history — blank for most stocks, and meaningful when it is not: wait) · and the Signal itself (Very Strong · Strong · Strong – Elevated Risk · Neutral · Weak · Very Weak). The group collapses as one unit from the View menu in Standard. The model is a list of plain rules checked top to bottom — the first rule that matches is the answer, and hovering a Signal cell shows the one rule that fired. The Weak and Neutral rules always sit above the Strong rules, so no amount of good news lifts a stock whose trend is broken. Backtested over 307,875 stock-days since 2008, the tiers order downside correctly (the worst tenth of 3-month outcomes: about −12% for the Strong tiers against −25% for Very Weak, before and after 2020 alike) — but median returns are flat across tiers. It manages risk; it does not predict winners.

Company Type

ETFs (no company financials) are scored on technicals alone. Everything else earns an establishment score out of 7: market cap ≥ $100B +2 (≥ $20B +1) · profitable +1, with a margin ≥ 5% +2 · positive free cash flow at a ≥ 5% margin +1 · forward P/E between 0 and 40 +1 · ROE ≥ 10% +1. Score 5+ (or $200B of size on its own) is Established; below that, Early — judged on growth, gross margin, cash burn and short interest, never on P/E or earnings growth, which mean nothing without earnings. A company migrates the day it earns it. A blank value never satisfies any rule, in either direction.

The rule lists

Established: Very Weak on a breakdown (more than 10% below the 200-day average and still falling: 1M < −8% or 3M < −15%) → Weak in a downtrend, or below the 200-day with weak fundamentals, or on heavy-volume selling more than 30% off the high → Neutral if below the 200-day, no trend data yet, under 200 sessions of history, earnings within 7 days, or extended (1M > +25%, or >15% above the 50-day, or RSI > 72) → then Very Strong (>10% above the 200-day, a clean entry — within −3%…+8% of the 50-day with RSI 45–65 — near the high and strong fundamentals) → Strong (clean entry, decent fundamentals, less than 20% off the high) → Strong – Elevated Risk (clean entry, fundamentals unproven but not weak) → Neutral.

Early: the same shape with a shorter leash — breakdown at −5%/−5%, weak fundamentals below the 200-day is Very Weak at once, merely being below the 200-day is Weak rather than Neutral, short interest above 25% is Weak, and Strong needs strong growth (revenue > 30%, gross margin > 50%, positive FCF) in a >10% uptrend. Early is never Very Strong. ETF: the technical rules alone.

Why this stock says what it says

Every Signal cell carries the one rule that fired — the phrase beside the verdict, and the cell's tooltip, name it. That is the whole point of a first-match rule list rather than a score: there is always exactly one reason, and it is written down. The four state columns beside it show the working, so the derivation reads left to right without anything to open.

A chevron before the word means the verdict moved since the previous session, and names what it was. Signal move in the bar filters to exactly those rows.

The trend ribbon on the stock page

Each stock's page paints a thin strip under the price chart: the Trend word, session by session, over whatever range is drawn — green stretches above the 200-day average, red where the breakdown rules held. Trend is the one part of the model that needs only price history, so the ribbon runs the full archive honestly. It uses today's thresholds and the stock's current company type throughout; hover the chart and the readout names the state under the cursor.

Each stock's page also carries a News card under the chart — the latest headlines for that company, dated and linking out to the publisher. Headlines accrue as refreshes run and when stock pages are visited; the screener's hover card shows each row's newest one. Headlines are collected by company name, so an occasional story about a neighbouring stock slips in.

The dashed trend-hold line beside the price (the TREND toggle, off until you ask for it) shows what holding only through the green states would have returned — in during Strong uptrend and Above 200D, in cash otherwise, each day's word acted on at the close it appears on. Flat shelves are the stretches in cash; the caption compares it with holding over the same span and counts the round trips, and the line carries its return as a tag at its end, green or red by sign. It assumes no trading costs and earns nothing in cash, so a high trip count is the warning that real results would be lower — the Single page is the full version of this experiment, with costs and sizing.

The dashed line trades one rule only: in during the green states, out otherwise. It deliberately does not replay the Signals — a full signal history cannot be reconstructed honestly, so the line claims only what the bars can prove. The model's verdicts still appear as explanation: hover a day where the model and the trend disagree and the readout names the blocking rule.

Why do these differ? in the ribbon legend opens a card below the chart that answers it with numbers: both rules' returns over the drawn range, then the largest stretches where one was in and the other was not, each named by the model's own flag — "Trend in — rules out: No clean entry (RSI 72)". Hovering the chart also names the blocking flag on any day the two disagree. The TREND toggle controls the line and only the line: switching it off takes the card, the return caption and the "If held" readout with it, because all three describe the line. The ribbon underneath is always there — it is a reading of the bars rather than a simulation, so it is not something to switch on.

One rule set — no custom set

There is one fixed rule set, the same for every stock and every reader. There is deliberately no custom version: a signal should always be explainable by one named rule. Collapse the Signal group from the View menu (Standard), or use a view that leaves it out.

Cushion — how far it can fall before the rules change their mind

Five Very Strongs on one screen leave you with a question the verdict cannot answer: which of these hurts least when it goes wrong. Underneath Cushion is a simple measure — the distance the price could fall before the technical rules flip that signal to Weak or worse, with the moving averages, RSI and fundamentals held where they are today. It is a gauge of where the rules stand, not a stop order, and it is the honest half of "risk/reward": the reward half would need a forecast, which this app refuses to make.

That raw distance is not a safety ranking, though, and it was tested. Across 307,965 stock-days of the archive, the stocks with the most room inside a signal tier were the ones that had run furthest above their own averages — 25.6% above the 200-day against 13.8%, on 31.7% volatility against 24.3% — and their worst cases were worse, not better: a 10th-percentile three-month loss of −18.5% against −15.7%. Room is extension wearing a risk label.

The Cushion column is that same distance divided by the stock's own volatility, in monthly sigmas, which makes a quiet stock and a wild one comparable. That version does order the downside: a thinner 10th- and 25th-percentile loss in every era and horizon tested, by one to three points. Sorting by the Signal column breaks ties on it, thickest cushion first — so five Very Strongs come back in an order that means something.

It ranks how bad the bad case is, never how good the good one is. Every average return difference in that study was noise, which is the same verdict the whole ladder gets: it manages risk, it does not pick winners. If you are choosing between stocks on one screen, cushion tells you which one is likely to hurt least when it goes wrong — nothing more.

Breakouts and volume

On the day a stock closes above its prior three-month high for the first time, a small dot appears beside its Entry word — amber when the day's volume ran at least 1.5× its own 20-day average (the confirmed kind, the one pattern in our measurements whose edge held both before and after 2020), faint when the breakout came on quiet volume (where the same measurements found nothing). It is a measured fact shown beside the state, not a rule — no signal rule reads volume.

When the signal changes

A small green or red chevron before the Signal word marks a verdict that moved since the previous trading session — yesterday's technical readings walked through the same rules, with the day-cached fundamentals standing for both days. The cell's tooltip and the hover card name yesterday's signal.

The Signal move picker in the bar filters the table by that movement: Upgraded, Unchanged, Downgraded, or Moved either way for both directions at once. An upgrade is a step up the ladder, Very Strong being the top. A stock with no signal from the previous close appears under none of the four — there is nothing to compare it against, which is a different fact from having held. The picker is hidden until a refresh has stored a previous verdict to compare with.

13 · Tiles

The Table / Tiles switch beside the View menu draws the same rows as cards instead of a table. It is the same selection either way: the sort, the theme, the sector, industry, signal and signal-move pickers, any screen and every column filter all still apply, so a filter that leaves seven stocks leaves seven tiles. Nothing is recalculated — a tile shows the values the table would have shown.

It exists because a short list wastes a table. Filter to seven names and two thirds of the screen is empty; as tiles those seven fill it, each with its own chart. Which shape you prefer is remembered against your account.

Per row, and the chart range

Per row is Auto or a fixed 2 to 6. Auto fits as many as the window holds; a fixed number is honoured until the window gets too narrow, below which the window wins — six across on a monitor is not six across on a laptop.

Range sets the window every chart on screen draws: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months or 1 year. It moves all of them together, deliberately — cards side by side are only comparable if they cover the same period, and it is one fetch rather than one per tile. There is no 1-day option, because the archive keeps daily closes and a single session would be a single point.

Each chart carries its own price gridlines, the dates at either end, and the move over that window with the window named beside it. That is not the same number as the change beside the price, which is today's.

What a tile shows

Which fields sit under the chart, how tall the chart is, and which extras appear — the verdict, the rule that fired, the trend word, the score chips, the sector — are one setting for the whole site rather than a personal one, so a tile means the same thing to everyone looking at it — the same screenshot means the same thing in two people's hands.

Tiles stop at sixty with a button for the next sixty: a tile is a great deal more markup than a table row, and 271 of them at once is not free.

14 · News

Headlines only — the headline, who published it, when, and a link out. No article text is stored anywhere, and nothing here is summarised or interpreted.

The News column

In the Info group, the News cell shows how old the newest stored headline is (2h, 4d), with a small count beside it when there is more than one from the past week, and brighter when the newest is under a day old. Sorted descending it puts the freshest first — it sorts on the timestamp rather than on the age, which is the same fact the right way round.

An empty cell means one of two different things, and the column separates them. A dash means the stock has been checked and nothing recent is stored. A faint dot means it has not been looked at yet. Headlines are topped up about a dozen stocks per refresh round, so the second case is common and does not mean a quiet company.

Click the cell and a panel opens with up to ten headlines, each with its source and age, each opening the publisher in a new tab, and a link to the stock's own page. If that stock has not been checked in the last six hours, clicking fetches it there and then; the news source is free, so this costs nothing.

The ticker

Under the table, a slow crawl of headlines published in the last 12 hours. It follows the table: narrow the view to one sector and the strip narrows with it, and says so. Hovering it, giving it keyboard focus, or pressing its pause button stops it; the × hides it, and News ticker at the top of the … menu brings it back. If you have asked your system for reduced motion it does not crawl at all — it shows one headline at a time instead.

On a single stock's page the news card carries a Check now button, which ignores the six-hour rule and tells you when that stock was last checked.

15 · On a phone

The screener is eighty-odd columns on a page that deliberately does not scroll, and it was never going to fold onto a phone. tickrlab.com/m is a separate page built for one hand. On a narrow screen the screener offers you a link to it; it is also Phone version in the … menu.

Four tabs along the bottom. Screens is the front page — every screen with how many stocks it holds right now, grouped as they are on the desktop. All is every stock, best first. Search finds one by ticker or company name. Tapping any stock opens a sheet with every field it has, grouped, and its headlines.

What a row shows

The View button at the top switches between a few named views — Move, Verdict, Value, and whatever else the owner has set up — each deciding which handful of fields sit under the price. The choice is remembered on that device. The views themselves are configured on a desktop; the phone only picks between them.

The chart button beside it turns the charts on and off, and the strip under the title sets their window. Turning charts off does not merely hide them: nothing is drawn and nothing is sent, so the page is as light as it can be on a slow connection.

The page does not refresh itself. Reload it and you get the latest data. Nothing is kept on the phone except which view you chose, whether charts are on, and which window.

16 · Cards to share

A card is a picture of something the screen found — the week's movers, a chart, what the rules concluded — drawn in the site's own theme at a fixed size, ready to post. Every one carries the wordmark, the date the data belongs to, the time the prices were pulled, and the line saying these are mechanical readings and not advice. Cards never recommend. They report what the screen and the rules did, which is the rule the assistant follows too.

Cards is the simple room: three cards, one shape, the controls in a bar above, and a Save image button. Promo Studio is the same machinery with more templates, three artboard sizes and a crop guide.

Getting the image

Download PNG renders the card at its true size — 1080 pixels wide at every shape — rather than photographing your screen, so there is no browser chrome in it and nothing to crop. A screenshot of the artboard works too, at whatever size your screen happens to be.

Saved posts

A card set up in the studio can be named and kept. A saved post remembers the template, the shape and every control — not a picture — so opening it tomorrow draws tomorrow's numbers. Saved posts appear on the phone under Posts, by name; tapping one draws it from the current data and hands it to you as a real image to press and hold and add to your photos. That is how a card gets posted without a computer.
