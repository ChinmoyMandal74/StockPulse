1 · What this is

Tickr Lab watches a list of stocks and scores them on how their prices are behaving and how the underlying companies are doing. It is a screener: it narrows a list down to the few names worth a closer look. The assistant deliberately never offers a verdict of its own; it can report what the Advice column concluded and the rule behind it, with attribution. The Advice column does carry a verdict, but it is the output of a plain rule set you can read in full in section 12 — a consistent reading of today's numbers, not a prediction.

Stocks are grouped into themes — a stock can belong to several — and the picker at the top left switches between them. Prices update after each close; the company numbers behind Quality refresh about once a day.

Why this exists

A brokerage terminal gives you four hundred indicators, streaming quotes and no opinion — the synthesis is your job, every time, forever. Tickr Lab is the opposite bet: one opinionated, fully-inspectable pipeline over a small universe you chose. Its defining feature is not any single number but that every verdict shows its work — click any Advice cell and you get the exact rule ladder, the one rule that fired, and the thresholds drawn as gauges. There is no "why exactly does this say Buy?" button anywhere else; here it is the whole design.

The second bet is institutionalised honesty. The backtests charge trading costs and hold out a period before looking; ideas that measured as noise are recorded so they stay dead; the trend line on a stock page refuses to claim any history the price bars cannot prove; and the nightly email says "prices only — fundamentals not re-pulled" rather than letting stale numbers pass as fresh. Even the watchlist carries deliberate ballast — faded names nobody is excited about — because a list made only of winners flatters every idea tested against it.

And the third is deliberate absence. No streaming quotes, no options chains, no order entry, no five-hundred-stock scanners: this is a daily-cadence research instrument, not a trading cockpit. It decides what deserves your attention and shows you exactly why — the last mile belongs to your broker.

2 · Reading the table

There are 49 columns, arranged into groups you can collapse: Info, Scores, Chart, Short-term %, Long-term %, Relative, Trend, Volume, Size and Fundamentals. The View menu (top left) decides what the table shows. Standard is every column, grouped, and lets you switch whole groups on and off; your choice is remembered against your account.

Column views

A view is a named set of columns you can switch to with one click from the View menu. Symbol and Name are always shown, and the columns keep the table's order.

  • Starter views (Trend & returns, Fundamentals, Advice) are shared by everyone. The lock beside them means only the site owner can change them — use the + beside one to copy it into your own views and change the copy.
  • Your views: + New view opens a list of every column with a search box; tick what you want, name it and save. It starts from whatever the table is showing, so trimming is quicker than building. The pencil edits a view, the bin deletes it. Up to ten.
  • The view you last used is the one the screener opens in. Filters on a column the view hides still apply (the filter strip marks them), and if the table is sorted by a hidden column a note beside the menu says so.

Getting around

  • In Standard, each group in the View menu has a jump arrow that scrolls that group into view — faster than dragging a scrollbar across 3,400 pixels.
  • ← → scroll the table sideways, Shift with them moves a page at a time, and Home / End jump to either end.
  • Click any column header to sort by it; click again to reverse.

The headline strip

Under the table, a slow crawl of headlines published in the last 12 hours for the stocks the table is showing — pick a theme, a sector, an Advice verdict or a column filter and the strip narrows with it — newest first, with each stock's move today. Hover it (or press pause) to stop it; click a ticker for the stock page or a headline for the article. Hide it with its ×, and bring it back from ⋯ → News ticker — the choice is remembered. These are stored headlines, fetched a few stocks at a time as the data refreshes, so they are recent rather than live.

Filtering any column

The Filter button in the bar opens a row of boxes under the column headers — one per column. Type in as many as you like; a row has to pass all of them. The button shows how many are in force, and a strip above the table lists them with an × on each and a Clear filters button.

  • Numbers: 5 means at least 5 · >5 <5 <=5 =5 !=5 · 2..8 for a range · 10B 500M for big numbers. A blank cell never matches. Scores read the 1–10 rating you see; Next Earn counts days, so ..14 is within two weeks.
  • Text (symbol, name, themes, guards): the text it contains · =NVDA for exact · !memory to exclude · chips|memory for either.
  • Lists (type, trend, entry, advice…): pick from what is actually in the table, with counts. Sector, Industry and Balanced are the same filters as the Sector, Industry and Advice pickers in the bar.
  • Esc clears the box you are in. Hiding a column keeps its filter working, and the strip says so. Filters reset when you reload the page; whether the row is open is remembered.

What is hiding behind a cell

  • Hover a company name for a card showing every column for that row at once — useful when the value you want is off to the right.
  • Hover the Qual. score to see the factors behind it, each with its weight and how this stock scored on it.
  • The 90d column is a sparkline of the last 90 sessions. Read the shape, not the level — each line is scaled to its own high and low, so two lines are not comparable.

3 · The Quality score

Where the return columns read the price, Qual. reads the company behind it. Six factors, all absolute rather than ranked against the list — a 30% margin is a 30% margin whoever else is on your screen. That is worth knowing for three reasons: a number means the same thing this year as last, adding or removing a ticker changes nobody else's score, and in a bad market the whole list scores low, which is the honest answer. A ranked score cannot say that — the best of a bad lot still comes out near the top.

FactorWeightMeasures
Earnings growth25Latest quarter against the same quarter a year earlier
Revenue growth20The same, on the top line
PEG20Price/earnings set against the growth rate — cheapness relative to growth
Profit margin15Net income as a share of revenue
Forward P/E10Price against next year's expected earnings
ROE10Return on equity — profit against the capital behind it

Companies without earnings

Three of those factors — earnings growth, PEG and forward P/E — describe earnings, and mean nothing for a company that is losing money. For a loss-maker they are excluded rather than scored zero, and the remaining factors are re-weighted to cover the gap.

This matters more than it sounds. The data feed will happily report a healthy-looking PEG for a company losing hundreds of millions, and taken at face value it once rated such a company 8 out of 10.

Sometimes there is no Quality score at all. If less than 40% of the factor weight has usable data, the column shows a dash rather than a number. A score resting on one factor is not a score. ETFs and funds show a dash for the same reason — they have no revenue or earnings of their own.
Missing data is dropped, not counted as zero. A factor that cannot be computed for a stock is removed and the remaining weights are rescaled to fill the gap. A stock is never punished for a number nobody has.

One click sets the filters, the sort and the columns that explain the result — the filters appear in the filter row and the strip above the table, so a screen never hides what it means. 5 · One stock in full

Clicking a company name opens that stock on its own page, in a new tab, with a price chart and every column the screener holds. The symbol at the top is also a picker — click it to jump to any other stock without going back.

The chart

  • Ranges from one week to the full archive, which for most names runs back to 2006.
  • 50-day and 200-day averages can be switched on independently. Each is drawn from enough extra history to start at the left edge rather than partway across.
  • Volume sits below the price, each bar green or red by whether that session closed above the one before.
  • RSI is its own panel with the 70 and 30 lines marked. Turning it on makes the chart taller rather than squeezing the price.
  • Move the pointer across for a dotted crosshair, the date on the axis, and a readout of price, day change, volume and RSI at that point.
The y-axis switches to a log scale when a range spans more than about four-fold, and says so in the caption. Over twenty years a linear axis flattens everything but the last few months into a line along the bottom.

6 · Asking questions owner only

Ask is not available to member accounts (since 2026-09-26). Every question hands the assistant the whole table, which is a large cost per question and is close to the limit of what fits in one; it is kept for the owner rather than removed.

Ask puts a question to the same data in plain English — "which names have a margin above 20% and are within 10% of their high", or "what is improving fastest". The whole table is given to the assistant, so it is answering from your actual numbers rather than from memory.

What it will not do

  • No buy, sell or hold verdicts. Not a disclaimer bolted on the end — it will not produce one, for the same reason it will not make up a number.
  • It says when something is missing and points you at what is there instead, rather than inventing a plausible answer.
  • "Will it go up" is unknowable, and it will say so rather than guessing.
  • General company knowledge is allowed, but flagged as background rather than presented as something your data shows.
There is a daily limit — three questions a day for members, sixty for the owner. It counts against your account rather than your browser, resets at midnight UTC, and a question that fails on a bad request does not consume one.

7 · How fresh the data is

The bar shows when the data was last pulled and the trading day it covers. Everyone sees the same stored snapshot, so two people reading the screener are reading the same numbers.

Two kinds of refresh owner only

  • Refresh re-pulls prices and recomputes the ranking. Seconds, and the company numbers are left as they were.
  • Refresh all additionally re-pulls every company profile — sector, market cap, margins, growth. It runs for about thirteen minutes, in batches, because the data provider only allows so much per minute.

A full refresh runs on its own every evening, after the close, so the list is current each morning without anyone touching it. While one is in progress every open page shows a notice, and the values already on screen stay put rather than blanking out.

A warning triangle beside a symbol means that stock's most recent price is older than the freshest one on the list. Usually it trades on an exchange with a different calendar, not that anything is broken.

8 · Your account

What is remembered

Which column groups you have collapsed, which view you were last in and how you had the table set up are stored against your account rather than your browser — so they follow you to another machine, and are not shared with whoever else uses this computer.

Your own themes

You can keep personal themes — your own named lists built from the stocks already in the screener. Click the star on any row to add or remove that stock, and find your lists at the bottom of the theme picker, where each one can be renamed or deleted. They are private to your account (the owner can review them), they follow you between machines like your column layout, and they never add a stock to the system — only the owner can do that, so ask through Contact if a ticker you want is missing. Up to ten lists; the dashed badges in the Themes column are yours, the solid ones are shared.

Emails

  • A short welcome when the account is created.
  • A password reset link if you ask for one. It works once and expires in thirty minutes; asking for a new one cancels the old.
  • Replies to anything you send through Contact, which reaches the owner directly.

The watermark

Data pages carry a faint, repeating watermark of your account email. It does not get in the way of reading — it simply means anything captured from your screen identifies the account it came from. The data here is for your personal use, not redistribution.

Signing in

Registration needs an invite code. With one you are in straight away; without one the guest preview is open to everyone. Change your password from the ⋯ menu; doing so signs out your other devices, which is the point. If you are locked out after too many attempts, it clears itself after fifteen minutes.

Something wrong, or a ticker you would like added? Use Contact — it comes from your account, so a reply reaches you directly.

9 · The Advice column

“Advice” here means the output of the fixed rules described below — a mechanical reading of the table, not a recommendation tailored to you.

The Advice column group shows the whole derivation, left to right: Type (Established, Early or ETF) · Trend (the price-trend reading — Breakdown, Downtrend, Below 200D, Above 200D or Strong uptrend: whether you may) · Entry (Clean, Clean near high, Extended or None: whether now) · Fund. (Strong, OK, Weak, or — when the data makes no case: how much conviction) · Guards (earnings soon, thin history — blank for most stocks, and meaningful when it is not: wait) · and the Advice itself (Strong Buy · Buy · Buy with Risk · Hold · Avoid · Sell Immediately). The group collapses as one unit from the View menu in Standard. The model is a list of plain rules checked top to bottom — the first rule that matches is the answer, and hovering an Advice cell shows the one rule that fired. The Sell, Avoid and Hold rules always sit above the Buy rules, so no amount of good news can buy a stock whose trend is broken. Backtested over 307,875 stock-days since 2008, the tiers order downside correctly (the worst tenth of 3-month outcomes: about −12% for the Buy tiers against −25% for Sell Immediately, before and after 2020 alike) — but median returns are flat across tiers. It manages risk; it does not predict winners.

Company Type

ETFs (no company financials) are scored on technicals alone. Everything else earns an establishment score out of 7: market cap ≥ $100B +2 (≥ $20B +1) · profitable +1, with a margin ≥ 5% +2 · positive free cash flow at a ≥ 5% margin +1 · forward P/E between 0 and 40 +1 · ROE ≥ 10% +1. Score 5+ (or $200B of size on its own) is Established; below that, Early — judged on growth, gross margin, cash burn and short interest, never on P/E or earnings growth, which mean nothing without earnings. A company migrates the day it earns it. A blank value never satisfies any rule, in either direction.

The rule lists (Balanced defaults)

Established: Sell Immediately on a breakdown (more than 10% below the 200-day average and still falling: 1M < −8% or 3M < −15%) → Avoid in a downtrend, or below the 200-day with weak fundamentals, or on heavy-volume selling more than 30% off the high → Hold if below the 200-day, no trend data yet, under 200 sessions of history, earnings within 7 days, or extended (1M > +25%, or >15% above the 50-day, or RSI > 72) → then Strong Buy (>10% above the 200-day, a clean entry — within −3%…+8% of the 50-day with RSI 45–65 — near the high and strong fundamentals) → Buy (clean entry, decent fundamentals, less than 20% off the high) → Buy with Risk (clean entry, fundamentals unproven but not weak) → Hold.

Early: the same shape with a shorter leash — breakdown at −5%/−5%, weak fundamentals below the 200-day is an immediate Sell, merely being below the 200-day is Avoid rather than Hold, short interest above 25% is Avoid, and Buy needs strong growth (revenue > 30%, gross margin > 50%, positive FCF) in a >10% uptrend. Early is never Strong Buy unless a profile enables it. ETF: the technical rules alone.

See the ladder for any stock

Click a stock's Advice value to open the rule ladder for that row: every rule in the order the model walks them — vetoes, then caps, then the buy setups — with the rule that fired lit and everything below it dimmed, because a first-match model never reads past its answer. Each condition is drawn as a small track: the shaded zone is what passes, the dot is where the stock sits today. The header repeats the establishment score as a seven-segment bar and the four state words. How is this computed? at the foot switches to the model itself — the full rule list per company type, thresholds only, no stock behind it.

Every other cell in the group opens its own panel too. Trend, Entry and Fundamentals each show that family's own ladder — the states in the order the model checks them, the one that holds lit, the same gauges under each. Type shows the seven establishment points and which were earned. Guards is the one family whose checks are independent rather than first-match, and its panel evaluates both rather than dimming anything.

The trend ribbon on the stock page

Each stock's page paints a thin strip under the price chart: the Trend word, session by session, over whatever range is drawn — green stretches above the 200-day average, red where the breakdown rules held. Trend is the one part of the model that needs only price history, so the ribbon runs the full archive honestly. It uses today's thresholds and the stock's current company type throughout; hover the chart and the readout names the state under the cursor.

Each stock's page also carries a News card under the chart — the latest headlines for that company, dated and linking out to the publisher. Headlines accrue as refreshes run and when stock pages are visited; the screener's hover card shows each row's newest one. Headlines are collected by company name, so an occasional story about a neighbouring stock slips in.

The dashed trend-hold line beside the price (the TREND toggle) shows what holding only through the green states would have returned — in during Strong uptrend and Above 200D, in cash otherwise, each day's word acted on at the close it appears on. Flat shelves are the stretches in cash; the caption compares it with holding over the same span and counts the round trips, and the line carries its return as a tag at its end, green or red by sign. It assumes no trading costs and earns nothing in cash, so a high trip count is the warning that real results would be lower — the Single page is the full version of this experiment, with costs and sizing.

The dashed line trades one rule only: in during the green states, out otherwise. It deliberately does not replay the Advice verdicts — a full advice history cannot be reconstructed honestly, so the line claims only what the bars can prove. The model's verdicts still appear as explanation: hover a day where the model and the trend disagree and the readout names the blocking rule.

Why do these differ? in the ribbon legend opens a card below the chart that answers it with numbers: both rules' returns over the drawn range, then the largest stretches where one was in and the other was not, each named by the model's own flag — "Trend in — Balanced out: No clean entry (RSI 72)". Hovering the chart also names the blocking flag on any day the two disagree. Switching the TREND toggle off removes all of it — the line, the ribbon, the legend and the card.

Five profiles, side by side — no custom set

The Advice group carries all five profiles as columns, so their verdicts can be compared row by row. Balanced is always the first — it is the shared measurement every other surface uses — and Trend Rider, Aggressive, Max Risk and Dip Buyer sit beside it. Collapse the Advice group from the View menu (Standard), or use a view that leaves them out. Each column carries its own colours, its own change chevron against that profile's previous trading day, and its own reason on hover; clicking a cell opens the rule ladder under that column's profile. There is deliberately no custom profile — a verdict should always be explainable by one named rule set.

  • Balanced — the defaults below. Loss-avoidance first. Always shown.
  • Trend Rider — buys strength sooner and holds it longer: chase caps loosen (1M +40%, RSI 78) and Early can reach Strong Buy. The stops stay Balanced.
  • Aggressive — more rope everywhere: stops widen to −15% (Early −8%), chase to +35%, blackout 3 days, Early reaches Buy on OK fundamentals.
  • Max Risk — the far end: stops at −20% with −12/−25 confirms, chase caps nearly off, Buy allowed to −30% below the high. Still never buys below the 200-day.
  • Dip Buyer — the one different opinion: a clean entry below the 200-day with OK fundamentals earns Buy with Risk instead of Hold. Stops stay Balanced. The research log does not endorse the mode; this column shows what it says anyway.

Distance to the exit, and Cushion

Click any advice cell and the ladder panel ends with Distance to the exit — how much further the price could fall before that profile's own rules flip the verdict to Avoid or worse, and which rule would fire there. It is the honest half of "risk/reward": the reward half would need a forecast, which this app refuses to make. Technical rules only, with the moving averages, RSI and fundamentals held at today's values — a gauge of where the rules stand, not a stop order. The hover card carries the same number under Balanced.

That percentage is not a safety ranking, and it was tested. Across 307,965 stock-days of the archive, the stocks with the most room inside an advice tier were the ones that had run furthest above their own averages — 25.6% above the 200-day against 13.8%, on 31.7% volatility against 24.3% — and their worst cases were worse, not better: a 10th-percentile three-month loss of −18.5% against −15.7%. Room is extension wearing a risk label.

The Cushion column is that same distance divided by the stock's own volatility, in monthly sigmas, which makes a quiet stock and a wild one comparable. That version does order the downside: a thinner 10th- and 25th-percentile loss in every era and horizon tested, by one to three points. Sorting by an advice column breaks ties on it, thickest cushion first — so five Strong Buys come back in an order that means something.

It ranks how bad the bad case is, never how good the good one is. Every average return difference in that study was noise, which is the same verdict the whole ladder gets: it manages risk, it does not pick winners. If you are choosing between stocks on one screen, cushion tells you which one is likely to hurt least when it goes wrong — nothing more.

Breakouts and volume

On the day a stock closes above its prior three-month high for the first time, a small dot appears beside its Entry word — amber when the day's volume ran at least 1.5× its own 20-day average (the confirmed kind, the one pattern in our measurements whose edge held both before and after 2020), faint when the breakout came on quiet volume (where the same measurements found nothing). It is a measured fact shown beside the state, not a rule — no advice rule reads volume.

When the advice changes

A small green or red chevron before the Advice word marks a verdict that moved since the previous trading session — yesterday's technical readings walked through the same rules, with the day-cached fundamentals standing for both days. The cell's tooltip and the hover card name yesterday's verdict, each profile's column carries its own marker, and both refresh emails carry an Advice changes list (on the Balanced rules, like everything else they report).

The Advice move picker in the bar filters the table by that movement: Upgraded, Unchanged, Downgraded, or Moved either way for both directions at once. An upgrade is a step up the ladder, Strong Buy being the top. It reads the Balanced rules, like the Advice picker beside it, and a stock with no verdict from the previous close appears under none of the four — there is nothing to compare it against, which is a different fact from having held. The picker is hidden until a refresh has stored a previous verdict to compare with.

10 · Tiles

The Table / Tiles switch beside the View menu draws the same rows as cards instead of a table. It is the same selection either way: the sort, the theme, the sector, industry, advice and advice-move pickers, any screen and every column filter all still apply, so a filter that leaves seven stocks leaves seven tiles. Nothing is recalculated — a tile shows the values the table would have shown.

It exists because a short list wastes a table. Filter to seven names and two thirds of the screen is empty; as tiles those seven fill it, each with its own chart. Which shape you prefer is remembered against your account.

Per row, and the chart range

Per row is Auto or a fixed 2 to 6. Auto fits as many as the window holds; a fixed number is honoured until the window gets too narrow, below which the window wins — six across on a monitor is not six across on a laptop.

Range sets the window every chart on screen draws: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months or 1 year. It moves all of them together, deliberately — cards side by side are only comparable if they cover the same period, and it is one fetch rather than one per tile. There is no 1-day option, because the archive keeps daily closes and a single session would be a single point.

Each chart carries its own price gridlines, the dates at either end, and the move over that window with the window named beside it. That is not the same number as the change beside the price, which is today's.

What a tile shows owner sets this

Which fields sit under the chart, how tall the chart is, and which extras appear — the verdict, the rule that fired, the trend word, the score chips, the sector — are one setting for the whole site rather than a personal one, so a tile means the same thing to everyone looking at it. The owner edits it from Tile setup, beside the switch.

Tiles stop at sixty with a button for the next sixty: a tile is a great deal more markup than a table row, and 271 of them at once is not free.

11 · News

Headlines only — the headline, who published it, when, and a link out. No article text is stored anywhere, and nothing here is summarised or interpreted.

The News column

In the Info group, the News cell shows how old the newest stored headline is (2h, 4d), with a small count beside it when there is more than one from the past week, and brighter when the newest is under a day old. Sorted descending it puts the freshest first — it sorts on the timestamp rather than on the age, which is the same fact the right way round.

An empty cell means one of two different things, and the column separates them. A dash means the stock has been checked and nothing recent is stored. A faint dot means it has not been looked at yet. Headlines are topped up about a dozen stocks per refresh round, so the second case is common and does not mean a quiet company.

Click the cell and a panel opens with up to ten headlines, each with its source and age, each opening the publisher in a new tab, and a link to the stock's own page. If that stock has not been checked in the last six hours, clicking fetches it there and then; the news source is free, so this costs nothing.

The ticker

Under the table, a slow crawl of headlines published in the last 12 hours. It follows the table: narrow the view to one sector and the strip narrows with it, and says so. Hovering it, giving it keyboard focus, or pressing its pause button stops it; the × hides it, and News ticker at the top of the … menu brings it back. If you have asked your system for reduced motion it does not crawl at all — it shows one headline at a time instead.

On a single stock's page the news card carries a Check now button, which ignores the six-hour rule and tells you when that stock was last checked.

12 · On a phone

The screener is eighty-odd columns on a page that deliberately does not scroll, and it was never going to fold onto a phone. tickrlab.com/m is a separate page built for one hand. On a narrow screen the screener offers you a link to it; it is also Phone version in the … menu.

Four tabs along the bottom. Screens is the front page — every screen with how many stocks it holds right now, grouped as they are on the desktop. All is every stock, best first. Search finds one by ticker or company name. Tapping any stock opens a sheet with every field it has, grouped, and its headlines.

What a row shows

The View button at the top switches between a few named views — Move, Verdict, Value, and whatever else the owner has set up — each deciding which handful of fields sit under the price. The choice is remembered on that device. The views themselves are configured on a desktop; the phone only picks between them.

The chart button beside it turns the charts on and off, and the strip under the title sets their window. Turning charts off does not merely hide them: nothing is drawn and nothing is sent, so the page is as light as it can be on a slow connection.

The page does not refresh itself. Reload it and you get the latest data. Nothing is kept on the phone except which view you chose, whether charts are on, and which window.

13 · Cards to share

A card is a picture of something the screen found — the week's movers, a chart, what the rules concluded — drawn in the site's own theme at a fixed size, ready to post. Every one carries the wordmark, the date the data belongs to, the time the prices were pulled, and the line saying these are mechanical readings and not advice. Cards never recommend. They report what the screen and the rules did, which is the rule the assistant follows too.

Cards is the simple room: three cards, one shape, the controls in a bar above, and a Save image button. Promo Studio is the same machinery with more templates, three artboard sizes and a crop guide.

Getting the image

Download PNG renders the card at its true size — 1080 pixels wide at every shape — rather than photographing your screen, so there is no browser chrome in it and nothing to crop. A screenshot of the artboard works too, at whatever size your screen happens to be.

Saved posts owner sets these

A card set up in the studio can be named and kept. A saved post remembers the template, the shape and every control — not a picture — so opening it tomorrow draws tomorrow's numbers. Saved posts appear on the phone under Posts, by name; tapping one draws it from the current data and hands it to you as a real image to press and hold and add to your photos. That is how a card gets posted without a computer.
