Area is market value and colour is the move, which is the only way round it can be drawn: contribution is signed and area is not, so a fall has no negative size. Contribution is the two together. The colour scale is fixed per window, so two of these cards are comparable; anything past it is drawn at full strength and counted in the note. Sized by market value rather than by index weight.

Each step is one company’s share of the market at the start of the window times what it did, so the steps and the pooled remainder add up to the last bar, which is the index itself. Begin-of-window weights: today’s weight times the window’s return overstates the index by 24.6 points over a year, because a winner has already grown into the weight being applied to it.

Each company against its own 52-week high: under 5% below is near the high, 5 to 10% a pullback, 10 to 20% a correction, 20% or more a bear market. The four indexes are measured the same way, from their funds. Sectors are ordered by the share of their companies 10% or more below.

Cash each company spent buying back its own shares over its latest twelve months on file, from its SEC filings. By share of market value a small company can lead; by dollars the largest companies do. A company that files no buyback line is not listed. The last column is what the share count did, since stock handed out as pay offsets a buyback. Square and Story show fewer rows than asked.

What a company's own officers, directors and largest holders bought and sold on the open market, added up and by person, from the Form 4s they filed. Stock received as pay is not counted. The card says how much of the selling was under a plan set up in advance: people paid in stock sell regularly, so a sale says less than it looks.

Call and put contracts open on one stock at the last close, in total and at the expiry dates holding the most, from the Options Clearing Corporation. It counts what is outstanding and does not say which way anyone is leaning: every contract has a buyer and a seller. Works for the index funds too. Refreshed each trading day.

The latest articles about one stock, each with an AI model's reading of how its content leans (−1 to +1) and its own two-sentence summary, under the week's figures. Read for the semiconductor stocks only; any other stock says so. A description of what was written, not a forecast.

Each semiconductor stock's average article tone over the window, one row a company: the ones with the most articles, ordered by their average. Articles that are not about the company, or could not be read, are left out.

Shares bought back and dividends paid, year by year, from the cash-flow statements the company filed, with the latest twelve months above. A line the company does not report is a dash, not a zero. Bought back is before shares handed out as pay, so the change in the share count is shown beside it. What was paid, not a view on whether it should have been.

The mutual funds and ETFs holding the stock, from each fund's own portfolio report to the SEC. The bar is the shares the fund holds; "of the fund" is how much of that fund this one stock is, not a share of the company. The reports are months old and not all as of the same date; the card says the range. Updated once a quarter.

What one manager or one fund held among the stocks tracked here, from its latest quarterly filing: the largest positions, the sector split, and what is new and gone since the period before. Only the tracked stocks, so the total is never the size of the manager or the fund. Open any holder's page and use its Visuals link to draw that one.

The latest fiscal year or the latest quarter, split the way the company tagged it in its own report. A fourth quarter is never reported on its own, so it is worked out as the year less the three quarters before it, and the card says so. "How the amounts have changed" draws the same columns in dollars, each as tall as that period's revenue. "How the mix has changed" draws one column a year or a quarter, each split into shares; a period the company reported on a different grouping is left out. Not every company reports all three; one it does not report falls back to the one it does. Subtotals are left out so nothing is counted twice, and the card says when the parts do not add up to the whole. The groupings are each company's own, so two companies' cards cannot be compared.

One row per holder, at the stake in its newest Schedule 13D or 13G from the past thirteen months. Not a full register: a holder with nothing to amend in that time is not listed, and nobody under 5% is. A fund has no card. Names are as filed with the SEC.

Each quarter's change in shares held by institutional managers (Form 13F), adjusted for splits, and the latest quarter's largest buyers and sellers among managers that filed in both quarters. Months old by the time it is public, and the card says so.

The Lines card's measures for one company: its share price rebased, its 30-day volatility, or a figure from its own filings. A price or volatility can be drawn day by day up to a year; a filed figure and a longer price line, one point per quarter end.

Each line is one sector. A share price is the equal-weight average of its companies' returns; revenue, income and market value are the sums of its companies'; margins, P/E and P/S are those sums over one another. Only companies with a figure in every point drawn are counted.

Each line is one industry, worked out as on the Sector lines card. An industry needs at least three companies with figures to draw a line.

The close in dollars, with the 20-day average (dashed) and the band two standard deviations either side, as on the stock page's Bands option. A measure of recent volatility; the card counts the closes outside the band and does not call them anything.

How far each close sat below the highest close before it. The deepest drawdowns are numbered on the chart and listed under it. The window cannot see a high from before it opens, so a drawdown marked "before" was already under way. For an index, pick its fund (SPY for the S&P 500).

The largest companies in the cut that report inside the window, under the day each one reports. The window starts the day after the data's own date, so "the coming week" on a Friday card is the Monday to Friday that follows. The bar is market value. An estimated date is 52 weeks after the same quarter last year, within a week nine times in ten; it is left off unless asked for.

Each bar is one company's short position on the two most recent FINRA dates, as a share of its float: grey is what was held on both, the colour is what was added or bought back. The figures beside it are before then after. Ranking by percent and by points of the float give different companies, since a position that doubled from 1% is not the one that added five points. The square card holds eight rows at most. Page 2 draws the same companies in the same order, so the two pages post as a pair: change the other controls and both follow.

FINRA publishes short interest twice a month and it reaches us about eight business days after it settles, so the card dates itself rather than implying today. The two rankings are different questions and in practice share no names at all — measured on the live fortnight, the eight biggest builds by dollars and the eight by percentage had nothing in common. The floor is on the POSITION, not on the move: a percentage of a tiny position says nothing, while the dollar ranking floors itself.

The two metrics are different questions and give different names — measured on the live screen, the S&P’s eight most shorted by % of float and its eight by days to cover share two names of eight. The year beside each one is the reason the card exists: the most shorted have a median year well below their cut, and some of them are sharply UP, which is the half a median hides. Heavily shorted and rising is the shorts under water.
