Checking the data…

The square holds five a side and a story fifteen, whatever is asked — a card cannot scroll, so a longer list is trimmed rather than printed over the masthead.

Both of these narrow the movers only. The sectors are the eleven Select Sector SPDRs' own returns — those funds divide the S&P 500 between them, so that block is already an index reading with nothing in it to cut — and neither the indexes nor the sector funds are ever counted among the movers.

Pick a sector to drill into its industries. There is no flat industry view and there cannot be one: there are 138 industries and the top twelve are 63% of the index, so the pooled tail would be the biggest ribbon on the chart. Inside one sector the same pooling is a real tail.

The left column is market value, the right is how each sector’s own cap-weighted return compares with the basket on the card. A sector that fell is Down whatever the index did, so green always means a sector that rose. The index funds and the eleven sector funds are left out of both columns — a fund that IS a sector would double-count it.

All sectors nests every company under its own sector. Pick one to nest its companies under their industries instead.

Area is market value and colour is the move, which is the only way round it can be drawn: contribution is signed and area is not, so a fall has no negative size. Contribution is the two together. The colour scale is fixed per window, so two of these cards are comparable; anything past it is drawn at full strength and counted in the note. Sized by market value rather than by index weight.

Each step is one company’s share of the market at the start of the window times what it did, so the steps and the pooled remainder add up to the last bar, which is the index itself. Begin-of-window weights: today’s weight times the window’s return overstates the index by 24.6 points over a year, because a winner has already grown into the weight being applied to it.

FINRA publishes short interest twice a month and it reaches us about eight business days after it settles, so the card dates itself rather than implying today. The two rankings are different questions and in practice share no names at all — measured on the live fortnight, the eight biggest builds by dollars and the eight by percentage had nothing in common. The floor is on the POSITION, not on the move: a percentage of a tiny position says nothing, while the dollar ranking floors itself.

The two metrics are different questions and give different names — measured on the live screen, the S&P’s eight most shorted by % of float and its eight by days to cover share two names of eight. The year beside each one is the reason the card exists: the most shorted have a median year well below their cut, and some of them are sharply UP, which is the half a median hides. Heavily shorted and rising is the shorts under water.

One measure, ranked either way. Themes and your own lists are under Within. The square card holds ten rows at most. Funds are left out of company measures, a multiple off a loss is left out of a valuation ranking, and the note on the card counts what was left out and why.

Measured on the live index the day this was built: 45.2% of the S&P 500 was above its 200-day by count and 75.6% by value — a 30-point gap, which is what narrowness looks like as a number. The size ladder says the same thing again: Mega 75.9%, Large 55.3%, Mid-Large 33.3%, Mid 12.5%. A stock with no 200-day average yet is counted in neither half, never as below.

The bars count companies, so the axis starts at zero — unlike the Evolution card, whose line may use its own range. The bin width is fixed per measure rather than taken from today’s data, or two posts a week apart would not be comparable. Where a measure has a long tail the end bar collects everything beyond it and is labelled with a sign; the note says how many are in it. Funds are left out — a distribution of companies.

The newest periods the company has filed, as many as you choose — a story holds eight at most, and the type steps down as the columns narrow. The bars start at zero; the margin line keeps its own scale, and every margin is printed under its period. A fund files nothing and has no card.

The peers are the Similar stocks from that company's page. Value draws the figures; Rank draws places, 1st at the top. Figures come from company filings, so a fund has no card.

A record, not a seasonal pattern: with about ten readings a month, one month always looks special by chance. The square card holds the newest eight years.

From the company’s own SEC filings — a trailing twelve months at every filed quarter, up to eighteen years. Market value is each quarter’s close times the share count the company filed that quarter, adjusted for splits since; where that history is not held it falls back to today’s count, and the card says which it used.

The window moves the chart alone — the eight figures are the stock's own fixed periods, and the range track is always its year. A verdict always carries the rule that fired and the rule set's name.
